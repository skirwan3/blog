## The business problem

Every tractor, combine and sprayer that John Deere builds is made of thousands of
purchased parts. A large share of them are **simple metal fabrications**: brackets,
plates, rails and weldments that suppliers cut, bend and weld from steel. Supply chain
teams decide which supplier makes each part and at what price, and they rely on many
part-level data elements to do it.

One data element they didn't have was an answer to a deceptively simple question:
**what should this part cost?** During my summer 2023 internship with John Deere's
Supply Chain Analytics team, I built machine learning models to answer it for simple
metal fabrication parts bought from suppliers in Europe.

Rather than a single number, the models produce a **price range**: a low, typical
(median) and high estimate for each part. A range is more useful than a point estimate
because real prices vary for legitimate reasons, and the question a buyer cares about is
whether a price is *unusual*, not whether it matches a guess to the cent.

<Callout title="Not a should-cost model">
  A should-cost model builds a price from the bottom up: raw material, machine time,
  labor. This model works top down, learning from the prices John Deere actually pays.
  That means it captures things a should-cost model can't, like premiums for supplier
  brand or quality, but it also means it reflects the market as it is, not as it ideally
  would be.
</Callout>

With a price range available for every part, several workstreams open up:

- **Cost savings:** compare each part's current price to its expected upper bound, then
  re-quote or renegotiate parts that sit above it.
- **Prioritization:** rank detailed should-cost reviews by their potential savings, so
  cost engineers spend time where it pays off most.
- **Supplier scorecards:** compare where each supplier's prices fall relative to peers
  on similar parts.

## The data

I joined three internal datasets at the part level: **schedule agreements** (the active
purchase contracts that record part, factory, supplier, price and expected volume),
**supplier metadata**, and **part weights**. After cleaning, seven features remained to
describe each part.

<PricingFeatures />

One cleaning step deserves a closer look. A handful of parts carried prices that made no
sense for their weight, usually due to data entry or unit-of-measure issues. To keep
them from distorting the model, I computed each part's **price per kilogram** and
removed extreme values using the **1.5 × IQR rule**: anything more than 1.5 times the
interquartile range (the spread of the middle 50% of values) beyond the 25th or 75th
percentile was treated as an outlier.

## How the model works

### Gradient boosting, in plain terms

The model is a **gradient boosted tree** ensemble. A single decision tree prices a part
by asking a series of yes/no questions — *Is it heavier than 3 kg? Is annual volume
below 1,000?* — and returning the average price of training parts that gave the same
answers. One tree is crude. Boosting builds many small trees **in sequence**,
where each new tree focuses only on the error the previous trees left behind. A
**learning rate** scales each tree's correction down, so the model improves in many small,
careful steps instead of a few big, overconfident ones.

feature, weight, so you can watch the prediction take shape:

That gap between training and test error is why the data was split before training: **80%**
of parts were used to train and tune the model (with 5-fold cross-validation choosing the
settings), and **20%** were held back as a test set to measure how well it prices parts
it has never seen.

### Predicting a range with quantile regression

A standard regression model predicts the *average* price. To predict a range, I trained
**three** gradient boosting models with **quantile loss**, each aimed at a different
point of the price distribution:

- a **lower** model at the 10th percentile,
- a **median** model at the 50th percentile, and
- an **upper** model at the 90th percentile.

Together, the lower and upper models form an **80% prediction interval**: for a typical
part, we expect the price to land inside the range about 80% of the time. The trick is
in the loss function. The upper model is penalized nine times more for pricing a part
too low than too high, so it learns to sit above most prices. Try changing the interval
width:

<QuantileBands />

The 80% width is a business choice, not a statistical law. A wider band flags fewer parts
with more confidence, and a narrower one flags more parts for review. The pipeline lets
the business adjust it later.

## Results

### How close are the predictions?

The plot below compares predicted and actual prices for test parts. A perfect model
would put every dot on the dashed diagonal. The shaded band is the 80% range from the
lower and upper models; dots outside it are either unusual parts or places where the
model is struggling.

<PredictedVsActual />

Most parts cluster under $50, where predictions track actual prices closely. The median
line bends away from the diagonal for the most expensive parts. There are few of them,
and their prices depend on details (material grade, coatings, machining) that weight
alone can't capture.

### What drives price?

**Feature importance** shows how much each feature contributes to the model's
predictions. All three models agree on the big picture.

<FeatureImportance />

**Weight dominates**, accounting for 47–68% of importance depending on the model. This
matched what pricing experts expected: heavier parts use more steel and more machine
time. **Annual volume** ranks second for the upper and lower models, consistent with
volume discounts. Quote type contributed essentially nothing; it was kept only so the
feature set matched other pricing models.

Importance tells us *which* features matter, but not *how*. For that, **partial
dependence plots** isolate one feature at a time and show how the predicted price
changes across its values:

<FeatureEffects />

Supplier and ordering factory showed effects of similar, modest size: most sit close to
zero, with a few slightly above their peers. Some of that may reflect sourcing patterns
rather than pricing, since difficult parts tend to go to the same capable suppliers
again and again.

### How accurate is it?

Two metrics summarize accuracy. **Mean absolute error (MAE)** is the average dollar
amount a prediction misses by. **Mean absolute percentage error (MAPE)** is the average
miss as a share of the actual price. They tell different stories, so it helps to see
them side by side:

<ErrorMetrics />

There was no existing pricing method to serve as a true baseline, so I compared the
model against a simple **$3 per kilogram** rule of thumb. On the test set, the median
model reduced dollar error by about 31% and percentage error by about 36%. Training error
was lower (MAPE 27.9%, MAE $3.70), a gap that's expected with this much
part-to-part variation, and the reason the test set matters.

of 3,072 parts with broader filters (any weight from 0.1 to 1,000 kg). Breaking the error

## Savings identified

The real test of a pricing model is whether it finds money. Once every part had a price
range, I flagged parts priced **above the median** and **above the upper estimate**,
then reviewed them with a cost engineer, who examined technical drawings and judged
whether renegotiation was realistic. For each flagged part, savings were sized as a range:
conservatively down to the upper estimate, and optimistically down to the median.

<Callout title="Result">
  Across simple metal fabrication parts bought from European suppliers, the model
  identified **$162k–$500k** in potential cost savings.
</Callout>

## Limitations and next steps

The model's biggest limitation is the data it can see. Weight is a strong but blunt proxy
for cost: two 5 kg parts can differ wildly in material, complexity, paint and surface
coating. Very cheap parts (where small dollar misses become large percentage errors) and
very heavy or expensive parts (where data is sparse) remain the hardest to price.

Separate experiments showed that adding **part dimensions and material type** reduces
error further. That data existed for only about 10% of parts at the time, so it wasn't
included. As those fields are filled in, and as users flag where the model's estimates
miss, the model should keep improving.

## Takeaway

<Callout title="Takeaway">
  Using only data John Deere already had, chiefly part weight, annual volume and part
  age, three quantile gradient boosting models produce a **low–median–high price range**
  for every simple metal fabrication part. The median model cut pricing error by about
  a third compared with a $3/kg rule of thumb, and its estimates match how experts
  think about cost: heavier parts cost more, and low-volume parts carry a premium. The
  model's real value is triage. It turns thousands of parts into a short, ranked list
  of likely overpriced ones, which surfaced **$162k–$500k** in potential savings in
  Europe and gives sourcing teams a repeatable, weekly-refreshed starting point for
  negotiations, should-cost prioritization and supplier benchmarking.
</Callout>
